CREDIT SCORING MODEL — TRAINING & EVALUATION REPORT
=====================================================

1. ALGORITHM
-------------
Model: LightGBM (gradient-boosted decision trees), scikit-learn API
(LGBMClassifier).

Why LightGBM:
- Handles hundreds of numeric features (here 349, after one-hot encoding
  and aggregation of the 5 supplementary tables) efficiently, including
  missing values natively — no imputation needed.
- Captures non-linear relationships and interactions between features
  (e.g. credit/income ratio combined with external credit-bureau scores)
  that a linear model like Logistic Regression would miss, which
  translates directly into a higher AUC on this dataset.
- Provides a native "gain"-based feature importance for global
  interpretability, and is fully compatible with SHAP's TreeExplainer
  for exact, fast local interpretability (no approximation needed, unlike
  KernelSHAP on a black-box model).

Feature engineering (scripts/preprocess.py):
- application_train/test.csv: ratios (credit/income, annuity/income,
  credit/goods price), EXT_SOURCE aggregates (mean/min/max/std/product),
  age and employment duration in years, anomaly flag for
  DAYS_EMPLOYED == 365243 (placeholder for "not employed").
- bureau.csv + bureau_balance.csv: per-client aggregates of past credits
  at other institutions (count, active credits, overdue amounts, debt).
- previous_application.csv: per-client aggregates of past Home Credit
  applications (approval/refusal rate, requested vs. granted amounts).
- POS_CASH_balance.csv, credit_card_balance.csv,
  installments_payments.csv: per-client aggregates of monthly repayment
  behavior (late payments, days past due, credit utilization).
All supplementary tables are aggregated to one row per SK_ID_CURR before
being merged into the main table, since the model is trained at the
client/loan level, not at the transaction level.

Regularization / overfitting prevention (scripts/train.py):
- Stratified 80/20 train/validation split (stratified on TARGET to keep
  the ~8% default rate identical in both splits, since the classes are
  imbalanced).
- Early stopping (100 rounds without validation AUC improvement) — the
  single most important safeguard for gradient boosting, since boosting
  keeps improving the *training* score indefinitely if left unchecked.
- Shallow trees (num_leaves=31), a minimum of 40 samples per leaf,
  L1/L2 regularization (reg_alpha, reg_lambda), and row/column subsampling
  (subsample=0.8, colsample_bytree=0.8) to reduce variance further.
- class_weight="balanced" to counteract the 92%/8% class imbalance
  during training (without this, the model tends to under-predict the
  minority "default" class).

Learning curve and stopping point (results/model/learning_curve.png):
Train AUC keeps climbing past 0.90 the longer training continues, while
validation AUC plateaus around 0.79 from roughly iteration 900 onward and
never recovers after that. Early stopping picked iteration 1611 (out of a
2000-iteration budget), i.e. ~90 iterations past the point of last
improvement — the gap between train (~0.896) and validation (~0.791) AUC
at that point is the expected, contained amount of overfitting for a
boosted-tree model on this many features; letting training continue to
iteration 2000 would only have kept inflating the train score without any
real gain on unseen data.

Result: validation AUC = 0.7915 on a held-out 20% stratified split of
application_train.csv (61,503 rows). This comfortably clears both the
minimum bar (0.55) and the target bar (0.62) set by the project.
predict.py reports this same number, since application_test.csv has no
TARGET column and cannot be scored locally — the test-set AUC can only be
read from the Kaggle public leaderboard after submitting
results/prediction.csv.


2. WHY NOT ACCURACY
---------------------
The dataset is strongly imbalanced: ~92% of clients repay their loan
(TARGET=0) and only ~8% default (TARGET=1). A trivial model that always
predicts "no default" already scores ~92% accuracy while being completely
useless — it never identifies a single defaulting client, which is
exactly the population a credit scoring model exists to flag. Accuracy
also depends on an arbitrary decision threshold (0.5 by default), which
has no reason to be the right operating point for a bank that may want to
trade off approval rate against risk differently.

AUC-ROC instead measures how well the model ranks defaulting clients
above non-defaulting ones across every possible threshold, independent of
class balance and independent of any single cutoff choice. An AUC of 0.50
is equivalent to random ranking; 1.0 is a perfect ranking. The project's
bar of 0.62 (achieved here: 0.79) means: pick a random defaulter and a
random non-defaulter, the model assigns the defaulter a higher risk score
about 79% of the time.


3. LIMITS AND POSSIBLE IMPROVEMENTS
--------------------------------------
Limits:
- The model relies heavily on EXT_SOURCE_1/2/3 (external credit-bureau
  scores) — by far the top feature in results/model/feature_importance.png.
  These are opaque, third-party scores the bank does not fully control;
  a large share of the model's performance is effectively inherited from
  an external, unexplained source rather than from proprietary,
  fully-interpretable signals.
- Missing values are not random: EXT_SOURCE_1 is missing for ~56% of
  clients, and several aggregated features are missing whenever a client
  has no history in a given supplementary table (e.g. no prior credit
  bureau record). LightGBM handles these natively but a missing value can
  itself be informative (e.g. "no credit history") in a way not made
  fully explicit to a reviewer.
- The local interpretability example (results/clients_outputs/
  client2_wrong_train.pdf) shows the model can be confidently wrong:
  SK_ID_CURR 435011 has a stable profile (44 years employed,
  EXT_SOURCE_MEAN=0.66, well above the population median) and was scored
  at 2.6% default probability, yet the client did default. This means
  some drivers of default are simply not captured by the available
  features — a real limitation to disclose to both regulators and
  customer-facing staff, who should not treat the score as infallible.
- Single train/validation split: the reported AUC (0.7915) is a single
  point estimate. It was not cross-validated, so its variance across
  different random splits is unknown.

Possible improvements:
- k-fold cross-validation (e.g. 5-fold stratified) to get a mean ± std
  AUC instead of a single split estimate, and to allow averaging
  predictions across folds for the final Kaggle submission.
- Hyperparameter tuning (Optuna/GridSearchCV) on num_leaves, learning
  rate, regularization strength, and min_child_samples — the current
  configuration was chosen from standard defaults for this dataset size,
  not from a systematic search.
- Deeper aggregation of the supplementary tables (e.g. time-windowed
  aggregates such as "behavior over the last 6/12 months" instead of the
  full history at once), which is the main lever known to push AUC on
  this competition above 0.78-0.80.
- A monotonicity constraint on selected features (e.g. forcing
  CREDIT_INCOME_RATIO's effect to be monotonically non-decreasing) would
  make the model's behavior easier to defend to regulators, at a
  potential small cost in AUC.
- Model calibration (e.g. isotonic regression) if the raw probability
  output is to be shown to customers or used for risk-based pricing, not
  just ranking.
